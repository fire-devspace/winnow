How you hold itPaper

Winnow · Puerto Rico · 15 minutes

One key. Two keys. Three.

Bitcoin on an iPhone, with no server anywhere in it. Your keys are made on the device, and nothing is ever asked which addresses are yours.

The rule underneath the whole talk: use the smallest policy that fits the money. A wallet you find annoying is a wallet you will empty into a worse one.

01 · one key

Open first. Synchronize behind it.

The wallet and its first address exist the moment you open the app, so writing down the recovery phrase never waits on the network. A fresh address can receive straight away.

This is the right shape for money you spend and the wrong shape for money you cannot afford to lose — one key is one point of failure, and no amount of care changes that.

For Q&A: what happens at payment time?

The receive screen watches a bounded relay window for an early observation. Confirmation still comes from a block. Spending shows the destination, amount, fee, inputs and change before signing, and spends opt into RBF.

A fresh address, seconds after setup. Nothing was asked of a server.

02 · the read path

Bring Bitcoin to the phone.

Peers serve public headers and compact block filters. The phone checks those summaries against your own scripts, on the device. It never asks a wallet server for your balance, because there is no wallet server.

For Q&A: what does a peer still learn?

A peer sees a connection and public chain ranges. A matched full-block request can add a correlation clue, so Winnow can separate peers. A full eclipse remains a real threat; this is a light client, not a validating full node.

03 · two keys

Two devices, and both have to agree.

Nothing moves unless both sign. Someone who takes one phone, or finds one recovery phrase, has nothing they can spend.

But the reason to reach for two keys is narrower than theft: it is the people who built your wallet. Hardware has shipped with keys an attacker could guess. Software has shipped with signing bugs. Either takes everything behind it at once, and you cannot audit your way out. Two devices from two different makers means one of them can be wrong and your money survives it.

And nobody has to know. Combined the aggregated way, two keys publish one 64-byte signature — no policy, no key count, no hint that a second device exists.

For Q&A: would this contain a weak-entropy hardware incident?

Yes, if only one independently generated key were affected. That key stays compromised, but the missing second signature stops the spend. Shared seeds, two affected devices, or a common implementation failure defeat the benefit — and losing either unbacked-up key locks a 2-of-2 for good.

The second device sees what it is approving. Neither can spend alone.

04 · three keys

Two of three, so losing one is survivable.

You choose how many keys exist and how many must agree. Hold two yourself and place the third with someone else — a family member, or a company whose business is holding one.

It also changes what you can be made to do. Someone standing over you cannot get the money out of you, because you genuinely cannot send it alone. And it outlives you: if you can no longer sign, the other two still can, after whatever real-world checks you set.

The trade is visibility. Spending this way publishes the policy — anyone reading the chain sees it took two signatures out of three. For the coercion case, that is the point.

For Q&A: what is not built yet?

Winnow constructs and spends the generic policy today, and two paths were verified on signet. What does not exist yet: packaged offerings from professional key holders, account handoffs, a provider directory, and production integrations with institutions.

Choosing how many keys exist, and how many a payment needs.

05 · match the policy to the money

Tomorrow's cash stays hot.

These are not three products you pick between once. One person runs more than one wallet: the till on the phone, the reserve behind two or three keys, and money moved up as it accumulates.

The mistake is not choosing wrong. It is leaving everything behind the one key you set up first because moving it felt like work.

06 · what the chain knows

Bitcoin counts signatures. Not reasons.

Winnow shows every valid pair that could move the money. Bitcoin does not know which one happened, or why — whether a loan was repaid, an owner died, a beneficiary qualified, or a court acted.

That gap is not a flaw to engineer away. It is the reason the real-world agreement has to be written down somewhere other than the script.

You + the second holder

Ordinary spending, and anything you can arrange directly.

You + the third holder

Recovery, or migration once an obligation clears.

The other two, without you

Inheritance when you cannot sign — and the collusion risk you accept by choosing who holds what.

07 · recovery, and one honest gap

A backup is more than twelve words.

Winnow's export carries your public history and scan state as well as your keys, so a replacement phone opens with your balance and past payments already in place instead of spending a long time rediscovering them. A phone swap was verified end to end on signet: balance, UTXOs, history, RBF links and scan height all matched.

The gap worth saying out loud: Silent Payment sending works. Receiving is off by default, because the mobile tweak-data backend it needs is still experimental.

For Q&A: why not compact filters alone for silent receive?

A Silent Payment output is not recognizable from the recipient's static code. The phone first needs a per-transaction tweak derived from sender inputs; only then can it derive candidate scripts and query the ordinary block filter. A provider could still observe timing or omit data.

A new phone, picking up where the old one left off.

08 · public beta, open source

Start with one. Grow deliberately.

Winnow defaults to public signet, so you can put the whole thing through its paces with money that costs nothing. Test it, read the source, or take an open issue.
